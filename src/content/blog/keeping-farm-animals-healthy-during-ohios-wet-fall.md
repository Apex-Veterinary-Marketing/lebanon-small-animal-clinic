## Recognizing Health Issues in Your Herd or Flock During Wet Fall Weather

As autumn rainfall increases, so does the risk for certain health problems in livestock and poultry. Recognizing early signs of illness is critical for preventing small issues from becoming major setbacks. For cattle, symptoms to monitor include coughing, nasal discharge, labored breathing, reluctance to move, swelling in the lower limbs, or changes in appetite and milk production. Sheep and goats may show signs such as lameness, scabby lesions around hooves, wool loss, or soft stools. Poultry are particularly sensitive to damp conditions; watch for wet, dirty feathers, decreased egg production, coughing or sneezing, and swollen feet or joints.

Producers should also be alert for behavior changes such as animals huddling for warmth, reluctance to leave shelters, or reduced grazing activity. In a wet fall, foot health can rapidly deteriorate, with lameness due to foot rot or other infections becoming more frequent. Youngstock and recently weaned animals are especially vulnerable, as their immune systems are less robust. Keeping detailed records of symptoms and promptly separating sick animals are essential first steps in herd health management during this season.

## Why Wet Fall Weather Increases Disease Risk for Livestock

The damp, chilly environment of an Ohio fall creates ideal conditions for certain pathogens and parasites to thrive. Prolonged moisture on pasture and in barns softens hooves, inviting foot rot in cattle, sheep, and goats. Muddy, wet bedding promotes bacterial and fungal growth, which can lead to respiratory infections such as pneumonia, especially when nights turn cold and animals bunch together for warmth.

For poultry, persistent wetness in runs and coops allows coccidia and other parasites to multiply, putting birds at risk for enteric diseases and bumblefoot. In ruminants, standing water and mud increase parasite burdens, particularly liver flukes and other gastrointestinal worms, as parasite larvae are more active in moist conditions. Additionally, abrupt weather changes can stress livestock, weakening immune defenses and making animals more susceptible to both contagious and opportunistic diseases.

Producers in the Lebanon area often report an uptick in lameness, respiratory illness, and digestive disturbances in the fall. Understanding these environmental risk factors helps guide effective prevention and management strategies tailored to Ohio’s autumn climate.

## Treatment and Management of Common Fall Illnesses in Farm Animals

When illness strikes, prompt action is critical to protect both animal welfare and productivity. For cattle, sheep, and goats showing signs of lameness or foot rot, treatment involves thorough cleaning of the affected foot, removal of necrotic tissue, and application of topical antibiotics as directed by your veterinarian. In some cases, injectable antibiotics or anti-inflammatory medications may be prescribed. Keeping affected animals on dry bedding and isolating them from the rest of the herd can limit disease spread.

Respiratory illnesses such as pneumonia require quick intervention. Veterinary professionals may recommend diagnostic testing to identify the causative agent, followed by targeted antibiotic therapy and supportive care. Ensuring animals have access to shelter from wind and rain is vital during recovery.

For poultry, addressing wet litter and improving ventilation are first steps when respiratory or foot problems arise. Veterinary guidance is necessary for selecting appropriate medications or supplements, as some treatments are species-specific and withdrawal times must be observed in birds producing eggs or meat.

## Preventing Weather-Related Illnesses: Practical Steps for Producers

Prevention is always more effective and economical than treatment, especially during wet fall months. Key strategies include maintaining clean, dry bedding and regularly removing soiled material from barns, shelters, and poultry coops. Providing raised, well-drained areas for livestock to lie down helps keep hooves and udders dry, reducing the risk of infection. Rotating pastures and minimizing overgrazing can decrease parasite loads and prevent muddy conditions.

For sheep and goats, routine hoof trimming and inspection are important for early detection of foot problems. Cattle operations should ensure that handling facilities, alleyways, and feeding areas are free from standing water and deep mud. For poultry, using sand or gravel in runs and elevating waterers can help keep birds’ feet dry.

Good ventilation in barns and coops is crucial for reducing humidity and airborne pathogens. Supplementing feed with appropriate vitamins and minerals can boost immune function, especially as pasture quality declines in late fall. Implementing a vaccination and parasite control program, designed in consultation with your veterinarian, offers additional protection against seasonal disease threats.

## When to Contact Your Veterinarian for Farm Animal Illness

Timely veterinary intervention is the key to preventing widespread illness and long-term losses. Producers should contact their veterinary team if they notice persistent lameness, sudden deaths, outbreaks of coughing or fever, unexplained drops in production, or multiple animals affected by similar symptoms. Delaying care can allow contagious diseases to spread rapidly through a herd or flock and may compromise animal welfare and farm profitability.
