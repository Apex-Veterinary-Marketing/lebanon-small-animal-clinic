## Recognizing Winter Risks: Signs of Trouble in Pets and Livestock

Understanding when winter conditions are starting to affect your animals is the first step in keeping them safe. For pets such as dogs and cats, symptoms of cold stress include shivering, reluctance to go outside, limping (especially if ice is present), and dry or cracked paw pads. Pocket pets like rabbits or guinea pigs may huddle, eat less, or seem less active when chilly drafts reach their enclosures.

Livestock face different challenges. Cattle, sheep, goats, and other farm animals may show signs of cold stress such as huddling together for warmth, standing in sheltered areas, shivering, or having rough, fluffed-up coats. Reduced feed intake, difficulty rising, or changes in behavior can also indicate that your animals are struggling with the weather. Calves and young animals are particularly vulnerable to hypothermia, especially during damp, windy November days.

## Why Winter Poses Health Challenges for Animals

Winter in southwest Ohio often brings a combination of cold temperatures, wind, rain, and occasional ice or snow. These conditions can stress both pets and livestock, leading to health risks. For pets, exposure to cold air and moisture can cause frostbite on extremities like ears, tails, and paw pads. Dry, heated indoor air can also contribute to skin and respiratory problems.

Livestock contend with more than just the cold. Wet weather increases the risk of muddy pastures, which can lead to hoof problems and increased parasite pressure. Wind chill can make even mild days feel much colder, especially for animals with short coats or those in poor body condition. Pregnant or nursing animals, young stock, and older livestock have higher nutritional needs and are more susceptible to cold-related illnesses.

Understanding these factors helps you take proactive steps to safeguard animal health as the seasons change.

## At-Home and On-Farm Steps for Winter Animal Care

There are many practical ways you can support your animals through the winter months. For pets, ensure they have a warm, dry place to rest indoors, away from drafts. Limit time outdoors on especially cold or wet days, and always dry your dog or cat thoroughly after returning inside. Paw care is essential; check for cracks or irritation after walks, and use pet-safe de-icing products if needed.

Pocket pets should be kept in well-insulated enclosures, away from cold windows or doors. Provide plenty of bedding, and monitor room temperature to prevent drops during the night.

For livestock, focus on shelter, nutrition, and water. Provide windbreaks or access to barns for protection from wind and precipitation. Bedding should be kept clean and dry to reduce the risk of frostbite and hoof problems. Increase feed as needed, since animals burn more calories to stay warm, especially during bitter nights. Ensure water sources do not freeze; heated buckets or automatic waterers help maintain hydration.

Keep a close eye on herd health by observing animals daily for changes in behavior or body condition. Address muddy conditions promptly to prevent lameness and reduce parasite risk. ## When to Seek Veterinary Help

Knowing when to contact your veterinarian is crucial for preventing small issues from becoming major problems. For pets, call your veterinarian if you notice persistent shivering, lethargy, or refusal to eat, or if you suspect frostbite or hypothermia. Sudden lameness, difficulty breathing, or ongoing vomiting or diarrhea in winter also merit prompt attention.

For livestock, immediate veterinary evaluation is needed if animals are unable to rise, show signs of severe lameness, refuse to eat or drink, or experience sudden weight loss. Calves or lambs that appear weak, cold to the touch, or are not nursing require urgent intervention. Persistent coughing, nasal discharge, or unexplained deaths in your herd should always be investigated to prevent the spread of infectious diseases.
